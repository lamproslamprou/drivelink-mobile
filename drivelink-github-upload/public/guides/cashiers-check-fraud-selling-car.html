Cashier's check fraud when selling a car

The dangerous part is not that the check is fake. It is that your bank will often make the funds available before it discovers the check is fake — and when it does, the money comes back out of your account.

Last updated 17 August 2026

Sellers ask for a cashier's check because it sounds like the careful option. It is issued by a bank, drawn on the bank's own funds, and treated culturally as the same as cash. That reputation is exactly what makes the counterfeit version effective.

Why the money can vanish weeks later

When you deposit a check, federal rules require your bank to make some or all of the funds available to you quickly — often the next business day. That availability is a rule about access to your account. It is not a statement that the check has been verified, and it is not a promise from your bank that the money is real.

Verification happens separately and more slowly. The check has to reach the institution it claims to be drawn on, and that institution has to confirm it issued it. For a good counterfeit, that can take days or weeks. When it comes back rejected, your bank reverses the credit and you are liable for the full amount, plus whatever you spent from it.

By that point the car is gone and the buyer is unreachable.

The sentence that matters

"The funds are available" is not the same as "the check has cleared." Tellers say the first one routinely. Ask specifically whether the check has been verified by the issuing bank, and be ready for the answer to be no.

The three versions you will actually encounter

The straightforward counterfeit

The buyer arrives, hands you a professional-looking cashier's check for the agreed amount, takes the car and the signed title. The check is a forgery, often a good one printed on genuine-feeling stock with a real bank's routing number. Weeks later your account is debited.

The tell: often none at the meeting. This is why verification has to happen before the handover, not after.

The overpayment

The check is written for more than the agreed price. The explanation is a shipping agent, a transport company, an accounting mistake at the buyer's end. You are asked to deposit it and send the difference to a third party — usually by wire, which is irreversible. The check reverses; your wire does not.

The tell: any overpayment at all, followed by a request to forward part of it. There is no legitimate version of this arrangement.

The remote buyer with a shipper

A buyer purchasing sight-unseen from another state sends a cashier's check and arranges their own transport. The transporter arrives promptly, sometimes before you have deposited the check. The car leaves the state, often the country, before the check is rejected.

The tell: a buyer who never sees the car combined with unusual urgency about pickup.

How to verify a check properly

  1. Find the issuing bank's phone number yourself. Search for it, or use a number from the bank's official website. Never call the number printed on the check — on a counterfeit, that number reaches the scammer's accomplice, who will happily confirm anything.
  2. Give them the check number, the amount, and the account it is drawn on. Ask whether that check was issued. Some banks will confirm, some will only tell you the account exists, and some will not discuss it by phone at all — which is itself a reason to move to the next step.
  3. Better: meet at a branch of the issuing bank. Have the buyer present the check at the counter and have the bank verify it in front of you. This is the closest thing to certainty available.
  4. Best: skip the check entirely. Complete the transaction inside the buyer's bank, with the funds transferred to your account while you are both standing there, or use an escrow service that confirms funds before you release the car.

Whichever route you take, do not release the vehicle or sign the title until the payment is confirmed by someone other than the buyer. The buyer telling you it is good is not confirmation. A document that looks official is not confirmation.

Things sellers ask about this

Is a certified check safer than a cashier's check?

No. Both are counterfeited, and both create the same gap between funds availability and actual verification. The distinction matters legally but not practically for your risk.

What about a bank wire into my account?

A wire genuinely received is final and safe for you as the seller — that irreversibility works in your favour here. The catch is confirming it actually landed. Do not accept a screenshot, a confirmation number, or a forwarded email as proof. Log into your own account and see the funds, or call your own bank.

What about peer-to-peer payment apps?

Most have transaction limits far below a car's price, and several allow payments to be reversed after the fact if the sending account was compromised. They are not designed for this and the protections do not fit.

Should I just take cash?

Cash is final, which is good. The problems are counterfeit notes and the physical risk of a stranger arriving with tens of thousands of dollars, or knowing you now have it. If you do take cash, count it at a bank and deposit it immediately, at the same branch.
